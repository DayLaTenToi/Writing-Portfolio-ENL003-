Poetic forms are like the basic Lego bricks, like grouping lines together, counting beats, making words sound alike at the end, and setting a rhythm that writers use to arrange their ideas and everyday language. Their main goal is to control how fast we read, create a steady beat, and make the feelings in the words much stronger. The shape of a poem acts like a carefully chosen box that can either squeeze the words tightly together to build a sense of nervous energy or let the words spread out loosely to sound just like a chat between two old friends. This guides how the audience reads and understands the main message.

Take Bashō’s “In Kyoto...” as an example. When I first read it, I was so surprised, as the whole poem is just three short lines. Turns out this piece uses an old Japanese style called a haiku, which is known for being very short by putting two different ideas side by side and focusing on pictures of nature during different times of the year. Translated into English by Jane Hirshfield, the tiny size changes how we read because it asks us to slow down and pay very close attention. Also, being so short leaves a lot of quiet blank space on the page around the words, and this space matches the sound of a bird singing out loud while also force the reader to think right away about the confusing feeling of missing a city even when you are already in the middle of its streets.

Additionally, the choices about how the poem is built are what help us understand this confusing feeling. The choices of where to start a new line create a tiny bit of suspense. Moving from the location "In Kyoto," to the physical sound "hearing the cuckoo," puts the reader in a real physical place before the last line shows the true emotional reveal: "I long for Kyoto". Also, even though there are no matching sounds at the ends of the lines, the piece uses the strong repetition of the word "Kyoto" at the start and the end to trap the speaker in their own feeling of missing the past. It shows how the shape of the poem physically brings to life the looping feeling of wanting a time that is already gone.

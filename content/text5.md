Most love stories are about two people. This one is also about a mountain. In "Brokeback Mountain," Annie Proulx uses setting not just as a background but as the heart of the story. The mountain and the world below it pull in opposite directions. One feels open and free. The other feels closed and crushing. That gap between them is what the story is really about.

In Ennis and Jack’s first meet, Proulx places them "above the tree line into the great flowery meadows and the coursing, endless wind" (Proulx 2). The words endless wind have two intention. First, they describe a real place in Wyoming, but they also say something about what that place means. There are no walls up here, and no one is watching. Proulx makes this even clearer when she writes that "there were only the two of them on the mountain, flying in the euphoric, bitter air, looking down on the hawk's back and the crawling lights of vehicles on the plain below" (5). The world below crawls and the mountain flies. The height is real, but it is also a feeling.

The scenes down below feel nothing like that. The cramped apartment landing where Alma sees them, the small trailer office where Aguirre gives his orders, the kitchen where Alma faces Ennis years later. All of those places are tight and small and full of people who judge. They hold the two men to rules that the mountain does not have. Even Proulx's writing gets shorter and harder in those moments, like the space itself is shrinking over time.

That is where the sadness of the story lives, that the mountain is not somewhere either man can stay. Every time they come back down, something is lost. When Proulx writes that "as they descended the slope Ennis felt he was in a slow-motion, but headlong, irreversible fall," she turns the walk downhill into the whole story in one line (5). Going down means going back to a life that does not fit. Setting is not just where things happen here, but is the reason things happen the way they do.

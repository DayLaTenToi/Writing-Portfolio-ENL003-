“The most terrifying thing Homelander ever does isn't use his laser eyes. It's decided not to.”

In The Boys, Homelander is sold to the public as America's greatest protector. He is patriotic, invincible, and most importantly, chosen. But through Michel Foucault's framework of biopower, he reads as something colder: not a monster who broke the system, but the system working exactly as designed.

Foucault defines biopower as the modern state's capacity to manage life itself, which is to "make live and let die." It operates through two mechanisms: disciplinary power over individual bodies, and biopolitics over entire populations. Vought International runs both. Their drug, Compound V, is injected into infants to manufacture superhumans, a literal biopolitical intervention on the body. Homelander wasn't born. He was produced. His entire existence is a disciplinary project: engineered, surveilled, and optimized from day one.

This lens sharpens the show's real horror: expendability. Ordinary humans without Compound V exist as what philosopher Giorgio Agamben calls bare life: biological existence stripped of political worth. When Homelander watches a plane of passengers fall rather than save them in Season 1, he isn't malfunctioning. He's enforcing the hierarchy. His gaze, the same one that can burn through steel, becomes the biopolitical gaze: sorting, assessing, deciding whose life registers.

Before this lens, Homelander was a metaphor for celebrity ego or creeping fascism. Biopower reframes him entirely. The Boys isn't a warning about a monster with too much power. It's a warning about the institution that would rationally, bureaucratically, create one, and the populations quietly deemed not worth saving.

The horror was never the heat vision. It was always the clipboard.

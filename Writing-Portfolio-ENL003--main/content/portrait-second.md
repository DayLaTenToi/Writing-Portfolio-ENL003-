# Writer's Portrait: Second Draft

## 1. Growth & Change
At the beginning of this quarter, reading literature felt like navigating an unfamiliar thing to me. As a student learning to read and write in English as a second language, I used to find poetic rhythm and abstract concepts difficult to feel. However, my reading have transformed. Now, I do not just read for surface meaning. Instead, I started analyzing the deliberate structural choices behind the words. In Maya Angelou’s poetry, for instance, I learned to identify how trochaic and dactylic meters, enjambment, and catalexis build emotional momentum and embody themes of resistance. Similarly, reading Octavia E. Butler’s *Dawn* through Murray Bookchin’s social ecology and Andrew Plisner’s ecocritical lens changed how I view literary pieces. I no longer see environmental ruin as a simple technological failure, but as a material consequence of hierarchical human social relations. These concepts have trained me to look beneath the surface of both texts and the world around me.

## 2. Your Writing Process
My writing process evolved from a stressful search for instant perfection into an iterative, structured journey. Previously, my language barrier made me dread the blank page because I feared making grammatical errors right away. Adopting Anne Lamott’s concept of "shitty first drafts" was a turning point. It gave me the freedom to get my thoughts down without self-censorship, trusting that clarity would come through revision. Feedback and peer review also played a pivotal role. For example, during the peer review for Essay 1, my reviewer Ava Mitchell pointed out that my introduction lacked context about my linguistic background and the poetry collection being analyzed. Addressing this feedback taught me to anticipate my audience's needs. I also learned to draft focused revision plans, narrowing my thesis statements and rewriting topic sentences to make my analytical arguments much more explicit.

## 3. Academic Skills & Transfer
Reflecting on the course learning outcomes, I have made significant progress in three areas. First, in practicing close-reading, I learned to engage with literary terms. Instead of simply stating that a poem was powerful, I analyzed how Angelou used catalectic lines in "Still I Rise" to create a confident, strutting pace, and how she mapped emotions onto physical structures through conceptual metaphors in "Caged Bird."

Second, in ethically engaging with and citing sources, I learned how to properly document my research in MLA format, creating structured Works Cited sections and using precise parenthetical citations to support my arguments ethically and build academic credibility.

Third, in responding to feedback and working through multiple drafts, I used feedback grids to evaluate suggestions. Because of that, I was able to craft writing better, and actually understand that good writers are the one who can improve there writing, not a first timer genius

Ultimately, the ability to close-read, integrate feedback, and organize complex arguments are skills I will carry into my future academic work and daily life. They teach me to communicate clearly and think critically about systems of power and representation.

## 4. Who Are You Now?
Re-reading my first draft, I feel a deep sense of relief and accomplishment. I started this class describing literature writing as my "weakpoint" and expressing how much I suffered wrestling with writing in English. Today, while English remains a second language that requires careful effort, my relationship with reading and writing is entirely different. I am no longer just struggling to get by. I have developed a genuine appreciation for the mechanics of language. 

By building this portfolio and materializing my understanding of feedback, coding, and literature, I have realized that writing is a creative and technological genre. I am now a more confident reader, a more "writer", and a critical thinker who understands that writing is never a finished product, but an ongoing process of growth.
